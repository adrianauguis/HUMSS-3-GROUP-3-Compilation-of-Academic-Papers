Review of Related Literature

Teachers PowerPoint Presentation Design and the Learning Experiences of Grade 11 STEM Students at Liceo de Cagayan University

Introduction

This section presents a synthesis of the existing literature on the group’s research topic. It explores previous studies, theories, and scholarly findings that provide context and support for the investigation. The review highlights key concepts, identifies gaps in current knowledge, and establishes the relevance of the research. All sources included have been critically evaluated for credibility, relevance, and alignment with the group’s research focus.

Design Factors That Affect Student Engagement

A PowerPoint presentation's success depends heavily on its design, understanding how specific visual choices influence student thinking and engagement is critical. Slide design is far more than a decorative choice; it serves as an active teaching strategy that directly shapes learning attitudes—namely attention, motivation, and classroom participation. STEM courses present unique challenges because they rely heavily on abstract concepts, mathematical equations, and technical diagrams. Poor design choices can quickly overwhelm students with mental friction, whereas clear, intentional layouts make complex topics manageable so learners can focus on understanding core principles (Han, 2024). Grounded in cognitive frameworks and classroom observations, effective presentation design rests on four key pillars: structural organization, text density, visual relevance, and spatial readability.

The way information unfolds across slides determines how easily students follow and absorb a lesson. When slides present ideas step-by-step with clear visual cues, students spend far less energy trying to figure out how concepts connect (Narayanan et al., 2025). Han (2024) notes that pairing intuitive layouts with structured cues organizes complex information, maintains student focus, and reduces mental fatigue during lectures. Establishing a clear visual hierarchy—using distinct text sizes, colors, or positioning to highlight key takeaways—allows learners to map relationships between ideas effortlessly. Aligning visual structure with Cognitive Load Theory guides student attention naturally and cuts through clutter that often causes learners to tune out during dense technical lectures (Paul & Seniuk Cicek, 2021; Kherfane et al., 2025).

Cramming too much text onto a single slide quickly drains student energy and divides their focus. When slides feature long paragraphs, students struggle between reading screen text and listening to the teacher, leading to cognitive overload (Paul & Seniuk Cicek, 2021). Ou and Ou (2024) found that keeping layouts uncluttered and focusing strictly on key points prevents unnecessary learning barriers. In local classrooms, Dancel and Lagura (2026) observed that structured visual aids and streamlined text help ease anxiety and mental fatigue among STEM students tackling challenging topics. Limiting slide text to concise, essential anchor points keeps students attentive and motivated without feeling overwhelmed.

Incorporating non-textual elements like diagrams, graphs, flowcharts, and short videos can boost student interest—provided every visual serves a clear purpose. Ansayam and Tan (2021) highlighted that strategic use of digital visuals directly aids comprehension and active involvement in Philippine classrooms. However, research emphasizes that graphics must explicitly support the lesson rather than serve as decorative filler. Unrelated images or poorly organized charts confuse students by creating false associations or unnecessary visual distractions (Kherfane et al., 2025; Wichanart et al., 2025). When visual elements align with the teacher's explanation, they reinforce learning across dual cognitive channels (Ou & Ou, 2024). For STEM learners, meaningful diagrams turn abstract formulas into clear mental models, converting passive viewers into active participants.

A slide deck's overall visual clarity, pacing, and design consistency create a comfortable environment for sustained focus. Wichanart et al. (2025) showed that adjusting slide design parameters directly impacts student attention and mental effort over long periods. Maintaining stylistic consistency—such as using uniform fonts, matching color palettes, and standard text sizes across all slides—provides a familiar structure so students do not have to re-orient themselves on every new slide (Han, 2024).

Additionally, giving text and images enough room to breathe prevents slides from feeling crowded. Squeezing complex visual elements like data graphs or molecular structures too close together blurs key details, leading to confusion. Ensuring readable proportions and proper spacing allows every student, regardless of where they sit in the classroom, to absorb information comfortably and stay engaged.

How a presentation is arranged affects how easily students can follow along with the lesson. Zhao and Watson Todd (2025) found that teachers who organized their slides in a clear step-by-step way and used cues to guide students' attention helped their students follow the lesson better than a teacher who just used a plain, outline-style slide. This supports Mayer's (2021) idea that a clear and organized layout lessens the mental effort students spend just trying to figure out how the information connects, so they can focus more on actually understanding the lesson. This concern also connects to Salic and Basmayor's (2024) findings, since their respondents admitted they were not very confident in using PowerPoint for different teaching strategies, which suggests that many Filipino teachers may need more support specifically in planning how their slides are laid out, not just in using the software itself.

Ultimately, slide design is not merely decorative, but also a vital teaching strategy that directly shapes student attention, motivation, and participation, especially in challenging STEM courses. And by thoughtfully implementing the structural organization, concise text, relevant visuals, and consistent spatial layouts, our educators can minimize cognitive overload and mental fatigue. Intentional presentation design transforms complex concepts into accessible mental models, fostering an engaging environment where learners can focus on core principles and achieve greater academic success.

Interactive Features in PowerPoint Based Instruction

A single image can make an unfamiliar concept easier to see, understand, and remember. In classroom instruction, images are not simply decorative additions to PowerPoint presentations; they can serve as meaningful instructional materials that help students connect new information with concrete visual representations. This is especially important in STEM education, where students encounter abstract concepts, scientific structures, processes, and relationships that may be difficult to understand through words alone. Abulkhair et al. (2024) demonstrated the potential of multimedia-based materials in supporting the teaching of heredity, while Istapra et al. (2021) examined the use of PowerPoint and pictures as learning media. These studies indicate that images can provide students with an additional way of accessing information and can support the presentation of concepts that require visualization.

The value of images becomes more evident when they are connected directly to the information being taught. Rather than presenting information through text alone, images can provide students with visual representations that reinforce important concepts and support recall. Williams et al. (2022) examined images and text on PowerPoint slides in relation to information retention, highlighting the role of these elements in helping learners retain presented information. Morita and Fukuya (2025) likewise investigated how learners process text together with multiple maps through eye-tracking, emphasizing the importance of visual processing when students encounter different forms of information. These findings suggest that images may be more effective when they are meaningfully related to the content and help students establish connections between what they see and what they are learning.

Images can also influence what students notice during a lesson. When a PowerPoint slide contains a relevant picture, diagram, or other visual representation, it may provide students with a point of focus that complements the teacher’s explanation. However, the effectiveness of images can be affected by the amount of visual information presented at one time. Bali et al. (2026) examined the cognitive cost of multimedia learning and found that visual load is an important consideration in learners’ processing. This means that images should be carefully selected and presented because excessive or complicated visuals may make information more difficult to process. Consequently, the educational value of an image depends not only on its presence but also on its clarity, relevance, and level of complexity.

Images can further contribute to the way students respond emotionally and behaviorally to learning materials. Interesting and meaningful pictures may make a presentation more appealing and encourage students to pay closer attention to the lesson. Rosyiddin et al. (2023) examined interactive PowerPoint media design in relation to students’ learning interests and highlighted the importance of presentation design in influencing learners’ interest. Similarly, the 2024 study Multimedia design for learner interest and achievement: A visual guide to pharmacology demonstrated the potential of multimedia design to support learner interest and achievement. These studies suggest that images may contribute to a more engaging learning experience when they are used purposefully and are connected to the subject matter.

The usefulness of images is also determined by how students themselves experience them. Flood (2025) explored the perceptions and experiences of students and lecturers regarding PowerPoint presentations and images used for teaching and learning. This perspective is important because an image that appears effective from the teacher’s point of view may not necessarily be perceived in the same way by students. Learners may appreciate images that clarify difficult concepts, provide examples, or make information easier to understand, while unrelated or confusing images may be perceived as distractions. Understanding students’ experiences can therefore provide valuable insight into whether images actually support their classroom learning.

Images may also help students make sense of information presented through PowerPoint and other learning resources. Lyu et al. (2023) investigated the use of PowerPoint in assessing students’ learning from multiple resources, showing the role of presentations in bringing together information from different sources. When images are appropriately connected to this information, they may provide students with visual references that help them identify and understand relationships among concepts. Sitompul et al. (2026) similarly examined the visual design of lecturers’ PowerPoint presentations during teaching and emphasized the multimodal nature of presentation materials. These findings demonstrate that images are an important part of how information is communicated in PowerPoint-based instruction.

Overall, the reviewed literature shows that images can contribute to students’ learning in several ways, particularly by supporting understanding, directing attention, strengthening information retention, and increasing learning interest. At the same time, the effectiveness of images depends on their relevance, clarity, complexity, and connection to the instructional content. Images that are intentionally selected and meaningfully integrated into PowerPoint presentations can become useful learning resources, whereas excessive or unrelated images may interfere with students’ processing of information. Therefore, examining students’ perceptions and experiences of images in teachers’ PowerPoint presentations is important in understanding how this aspect of presentation design may influence the learning attitudes of Grade 11 STEM students.
